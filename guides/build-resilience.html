How to Build Resilience: 9 Evidence-Based Strategies

Resilience is a skill you can build. Nine strategies from psychology research to bounce back from setbacks and grow through challenges.

Resilience isn't a personality trait you either have or don't. The American Psychological Association describes it as a process of adapting well in the face of adversity, and it draws on behaviours, thoughts and actions anyone can learn and develop.

1. Build your connection network

Strong relationships are one of the most consistent protective factors in resilience research. Invest in a handful of people you can call on a bad day, and be that person for them.

2. Look after the basics

Sleep, movement and nutrition aren't optional extras. They're the physical capacity your mind runs on. Stress hits harder when you're running on empty.

3. Accept what you can't control

The Stoics split life into what's "up to us" and what isn't. Make two lists for any problem. Put your energy into the first.

4. Reframe the story

How you explain setbacks shapes how you recover. Practise seeing them as temporary, specific and survivable. The thought reframer helps.

5. Keep moving toward goals

Small, realistic steps restore a sense of agency. Ask, "What's one thing I can do today that moves me forward?"

6. Find meaning

Viktor Frankl wrote that people can endure almost anything if they find meaning in it. Ask what this challenge could teach you, or how you might use it to help others.

7. Practise self-compassion

Kristin Neff's research suggests that treating yourself with the kindness you'd show a friend is linked to greater resilience and motivation, not complacency.

8. Train with small stresses

Doing manageable hard things, like a tough workout, a difficult conversation or learning a skill, builds confidence that you can handle discomfort.

9. Ask for help early

Resilient people aren't lone wolves. They use their resources. That might be a friend, a coach for forward momentum, or a therapist when life gets heavy.

Resilience doesn't mean you won't feel pain. It means you'll be able to move through it. Be patient with yourself.

Frequently asked questions

Can resilience be learned?

Yes. Psychologists describe resilience as a set of skills and habits, including connection, self-care, reframing and goal-directed action, that can be developed over time.

What are the pillars of resilience?

Common frameworks include connection, wellness, healthy thinking and meaning or purpose.
